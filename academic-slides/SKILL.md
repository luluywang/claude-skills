# Academic Slide Style

Apply these preferences alongside the presentation skill's authoring and verification workflow. Follow the user's current edits and explicit instructions over these defaults. Treat this as a style guide, not a fixed visual template.

## Organize an academic argument

- Start with the economic question and tension. Preview the contribution and findings, connect the relevant literatures, then present the institutional setting, data and key empirical facts. Adapt this order when the audience needs a short definition first.
- Assume a technically sophisticated economics audience. Explain the institution and information structure, rather than padding the deck with generic background.
- Distinguish what participants gain from effects on outsiders. When relevant, explain how efficient cooperation can create externalities without requiring agency conflict or misconduct.
- Keep proposed results and unestimated mechanisms identifiable as such. Treat confident wording in a dummy presentation as draft language, not evidence that estimates exist.

## Maximize substantive density

- Put enough evidence, definitions and economic interpretation on a slide to support a real argument. Avoid spreading a single thought across several sparse slides.
- Use one dominant exhibit for each empirical concept. Consolidate close variants of the same fact instead of creating separate slides for every statistic, year or subgroup.
- Use multiple series, categories, stages, or carefully chosen panels within that exhibit when they answer the same question. Interpret “one chart per concept” as conceptual integration, not a ban on comparisons.
- Split slides when they address distinct economic questions or when legibility requires it. Avoid replacing density with tiny type or an unreadable dashboard.
- Prefer one large chart with several meaningful comparisons to a prose-only setup, a chart, a second near-duplicate chart and a takeaway slide.
- Preserve useful institutional quotes and precise data definitions. Remove repeated narration of what a chart already shows.

## Make the chart carry the finding

- Write a concrete, defensible title that identifies the fact or question. Avoid promotional slogans, decorative kickers and generic takeaway boxes.
- Give the figure most of the slide. Keep the source and essential sample qualifications compact. Put full methods, URLs and secondary caveats in notes.
- Use time series to establish growth and prevalence when available. For an additive decomposition, put components and the comparison group together in one exhibit.
- For example, compare private-origin homes' private plus public duration against directly public homes in one chart across price bands. Avoid separate mean-DOM and median-DOM slides that repeat the same conceptual point.
- Preserve denominators, sample dates, units and status definitions. Distinguish direct-to-public listings, private-to-public transitions and private closings.
- Stack means only when populations and endpoints support addition. Do not silently sum medians. If an illustrative stack uses reported component means with uncertain cohort alignment, label that limitation.
- Use external market evidence to fill a motivating gap when authorized. Identify the market and limitation visibly, and state what should later be recomputed in the focal data.
- Never fill gaps with invented estimates or turn descriptive associations into causal findings.

## Write compact academic prose

- Use direct sentences and economical bullets. Allow substantive bullets and equations when they advance the argument.
- Write every bullet list and every series of slide titles to `writing_standards/list_readability.md`: start with the subject or verb, put the distinguishing words first, keep one structure and one name per concept, and vary an item only when the difference is real.
- Avoid fluffy introductions, rhetorical filler, repeated summaries and long prose beneath a self-explanatory figure.
- Use text slides for the research question, model, data and conceptual synthesis. Do not force every slide into a chart.
- Prefer plain white backgrounds, prominent dark titles, readable body text and restrained color. Let the user's current deck determine exact typography and layout.
- Do not impose corporate cards, icons, stock illustrations or elaborate visual branding on a research talk.

## Synthesize the literature

- Default to one dense literature slide organized around the small number of mechanisms that jointly motivate the paper.
- Cite representative papers inline, explain each strand's insight briefly, then articulate the bridge and contribution.
- For example, merge platform and contracting externalities with housing learning and DOM signaling: platform choice determines the history buyers observe and can change beliefs about nonparticipants.
- Avoid a slide per paper, disconnected citation inventories, or multiplying adjacent literature slides without a clear need.
- Verify bibliographic details and what a paper actually establishes. Retain user-supplied citations without inventing missing titles or findings. Keep a fuller bibliography in notes or backup.

## Edit collaboratively

- Inspect the current artifact immediately before editing. The user may have changed slide order, titles, text and formatting since the last turn.
- Edit the requested slide in place and preserve the user's wording and structure where possible. Do not overwrite their revisions with an earlier draft.
- Reorder or consolidate evidence to strengthen the argument. Keep useful backup material separate from the main talk rather than letting it dictate the sequence.
- Render edited slides and check readability, source qualifications and chart comparisons. Verify that the main point survives without accompanying narration.

## Final editorial check

Ask:
1. What economic question does this slide answer?
2. Does each element add evidence, define the sample, or explain a mechanism?
3. Can adjacent slides on the same concept become one stronger exhibit?
4. Does the prose repeat the chart?
5. Can the audience read the slide at presentation size?
6. Are proposed findings, descriptive facts and causal claims clearly distinguished?
